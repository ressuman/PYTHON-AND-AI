# Ghanaian Food & Culinary Tourism

## Signature Ghanaian Dishes

### Jollof Rice
The iconic West African rice dish, slow-cooked in tomato sauce with onions, peppers,
and spices. Ghana's version is distinct from Nigerian jollof — typically smokier due
to the "party jollof" technique of allowing the bottom of the pot to crust slightly,
producing a smoky caramelised base layer. Chicken jollof is the most common variant.
Available everywhere — street stalls, chop bars, and fine dining restaurants alike.
A meal no visitor should leave Ghana without trying.

### Waakye
A popular Ghanaian breakfast and lunch staple made from rice and black-eyed peas
cooked together until the beans turn the rice a distinctive reddish-brown colour.
Served with shito (black pepper sauce), fried or grilled fish, boiled eggs, avocado,
spaghetti, and gari (cassava granules). The combination sounds unusual but works
perfectly together. The iconic waakye line at Auntie Muni's spot in Accra stretches
around the block by 7am — a sign of how beloved this dish is.

### Fufu
Made by pounding boiled cassava and plantain (or yam) into a smooth, elastic dough.
Served in a broth — most commonly light soup with chicken or fish, or palm nut soup
(richer, redder, more aromatic). The correct eating technique: tear small balls with
your right hand, dip in the soup, and swallow without chewing. The texture is the
experience. Available at most traditional chop bars and restaurants across Ghana.

### Kenkey
A fermented corn dumpling steamed in corn husks, similar to a tamale in texture.
Ga kenkey (from the Ga people of Accra) is a staple of coastal Ghana. Served with
grilled tilapia or fried fish, fresh pepper sauce, and raw onion. Famous for its sour,
fermented flavour profile — an acquired taste that many visitors come to love.
Fante kenkey (from Cape Coast) is a slightly different variant, wrapped differently
and less sour.

### Banku
Made from fermented corn and cassava dough, cooked on the stovetop into a smooth,
slightly elastic ball. Served with tilapia, okro (okra) soup, or grilled chicken.
Popular across the Volta and Greater Accra regions. Often eaten with the hands —
tear a piece, make an indentation with your thumb to scoop the soup.

### Kelewele
Spiced fried plantain — cut into cubes, marinated with fresh ginger, aniseed,
and chilli, then deep-fried until caramelised and slightly crispy on the outside.
A beloved street snack sold at night markets throughout Ghana, particularly popular
in Accra's Osu and Lapaz neighbourhoods after dark. Often sold alongside roasted
groundnuts (peanuts).

### Kontomire Stew (Palava Sauce)
A rich stew made from cocoyam leaves (similar to spinach), smoked fish, and palm oil.
Served with boiled yam, plantain, or rice. Nutritious and deeply satisfying.
A home-cooking staple that represents everyday Ghanaian family meals.

### Red Red
A dish of fried plantain served with black-eyed bean stew cooked in palm oil —
the "red red" name comes from the double red of the palm oil and the ripe plantain.
Simple, inexpensive, and absolutely delicious. Popular at chop bars and street stalls.

## Street Food & Snacks
- **Roasted plantain and groundnuts** — sold on roadsides everywhere, particularly
  in the evenings. A perfect combination of sweet and savoury.
- **Meat pies and egg rolls** — bakery staples sold at roadside kiosks.
- **Bofrot (puff puff)** — deep-fried dough balls, slightly sweet. West Africa's
  answer to the doughnut. Sold by street vendors in the mornings.
- **Sobolo (Hibiscus drink)** — deep red drink made from dried hibiscus flowers,
  ginger, and sugar. Refreshing and widely available. Ghana's version of bissap.
- **Fresh coconut** — sold chilled by roadside vendors. Vendor cracks it open
  and provides a straw for the water, then splits it for the flesh.

## Culinary Tourism Experiences

### Chop Bars
Traditional Ghanaian food courts — informal restaurants serving local dishes at
very affordable prices (GHS 25–60 per full meal). An essential cultural experience.
Look for the busy ones with a queue — high turnover means the food is fresh.
Many have no signage; follow the smell and the crowd.

### Food Markets
**Makola Market (Accra)** — the city's largest and busiest market. Noisy, crowded,
full of energy, and a genuine sensory experience. Excellent fresh produce, dried fish,
spices, and snacks. Keep belongings secure.

**Kumasi Central Market** — one of the largest open-air markets in West Africa.
The spice section alone is worth the visit — piles of dried chillies, prekese,
grains of selim, and other aromatics used in Ghanaian cooking.

### Restaurant Scene in Accra
Accra's restaurant scene has expanded significantly. Notable options:
- **Buka** — upscale West African cuisine, popular with the diaspora community
- **Jerrymore** — traditional Ghanaian dishes in a comfortable setting
- **The View Bar & Grill** — open-air rooftop, grilled meats and local dishes
- **Santoku** — consistently rated best Japanese restaurant in West Africa
  (for comparison and variety during longer stays)
- **Nkosenkyem** — authentic Ghanaian food in a cultural setting near Osu

## Food Safety Tips
- Drink bottled or treated water only. Do not drink tap water.
- Ice in restaurants may or may not be from treated water — ask, or avoid.
- Street food is generally safe if the vendor is visibly busy — high turnover
  means ingredients are fresh and food is not sitting out for hours.
- Avoid pre-cut fruit from street vendors in open displays.
- Wash hands before eating, especially if eating with your hands (which is
  the traditional and correct way for fufu, banku, and many other dishes).
- Shellfish from roadside vendors should be approached with caution.
  Cooked shellfish from reputable restaurants is generally fine.
