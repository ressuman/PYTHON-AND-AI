# Endangered Languages in Ghana

## Scale of Linguistic Diversity
Ghana is home to approximately 80 distinct languages from three major families:
- **Niger-Congo — Kwa branch:** Akan languages (Twi, Fante, Akuapem), Ga, Dangme,
  Ewe, Nzema, and many smaller languages of southern Ghana
- **Niger-Congo — Gur (Voltaic) branch:** Dagbani, Dagaare, Gurene (Frafra),
  Kasem, Gonja, Konkomba, and many others of northern Ghana
- **Niger-Congo — Mande branch:** Ligbi, Deg (Mo), and a few smaller languages
- **Nilo-Saharan:** A small number of languages in extreme northern Ghana

Of these 80 languages, a significant proportion are spoken by communities of
fewer than 10,000 people and face various degrees of endangerment due to language
shift — speakers (particularly younger generations) adopting Twi, English, or
other regional languages in place of their heritage language.

## UNESCO Language Endangerment Scale
UNESCO categorises languages on a scale from Vulnerable to Extinct:
- **Vulnerable:** Most children speak it, but use may be restricted to certain domains
- **Definitely endangered:** Children no longer learn the language at home
- **Severely endangered:** Language spoken by grandparents; parents may understand but
  do not use with children; children do not speak it
- **Critically endangered:** Youngest speakers are grandparents and older; spoken rarely
- **Extinct:** No speakers remain

Several Ghanaian languages fall into the Severely and Critically Endangered categories.

## Notable Endangered Languages in Ghana

### Nkonya
- Region: Oti Region, Volta Basin
- Speakers: approximately 15,000–25,000
- Family: Kwa (Guang subgroup)
- Status: Vulnerable to Definitely Endangered
- Notes: Facing increasing language shift toward Twi among younger speakers.
  Limited written materials exist. Documentation work has been conducted by
  SIL International linguists.

### Siwu (Akpafu-Lolobi)
- Region: Oti Region, Volta Basin
- Speakers: approximately 20,000–30,000
- Family: Kwa (Guang subgroup)
- Status: Vulnerable
- Notes: Subject of notable linguistic research by the Max Planck Institute
  for Psycholinguistics (Nijmegen, Netherlands), particularly on ideophones
  (sound-symbolic words that mimic sensory experiences) and the relationship
  between gesture and speech. The Siwu language has an unusually rich ideophone
  system that has attracted international linguistic attention.

### Animere
- Region: Volta Region, near Hohoe
- Speakers: Very few (critically endangered)
- Family: Kwa
- Status: Critically Endangered
- Notes: One of Ghana's most endangered languages. Documentation is urgent.

### Nkoranza Bono
- Region: Bono East Region
- Status: Varieties showing divergence and shift toward standard Asante Twi
- Notes: Represents a dialect-to-language-death continuum situation where
  a once-distinct variety is merging into the dominant related language.

### Deg (Mo)
- Region: Savannah Region
- Speakers: approximately 40,000
- Family: Mande
- Status: Vulnerable (the most widely spoken Mande language in Ghana but
  still small relative to Akan and Gur languages)

### Vagla
- Region: Savannah Region
- Speakers: approximately 10,000
- Family: Gur
- Status: Definitely Endangered

## Causes of Language Endangerment in Ghana
1. **Urbanisation** — Rural communities speaking smaller languages move to cities
   where Twi and English are needed for daily life; children grow up as Twi/English
   speakers rather than heritage language speakers
2. **Economic pressure** — Perceived lack of economic value in minority languages
   versus Twi (for local markets) or English (for formal employment)
3. **Education system** — Schools teaching in English and/or Twi give children
   little reason or opportunity to develop literacy in heritage languages
4. **Media** — Radio, television, social media, and entertainment predominantly
   in English, Twi, and other major languages; minority languages have minimal
   media presence
5. **Intermarriage** — Couples from different language backgrounds default to
   Twi or English in the home; children grow up without a clear heritage language
6. **Attitudes** — Some speakers develop negative attitudes toward their own
   heritage languages, viewing them as symbols of rural backwardness rather than
   valued cultural inheritance

## Documentation and Preservation Efforts
**University of Ghana, Department of Linguistics:** Active research and documentation
programmes for multiple Ghanaian languages. Produces grammars, dictionaries, and
archival recordings.

**SIL International (Ghana):** Long-running documentation and literacy programmes
for many minority languages of Ghana, particularly in northern Ghana.

**Endangered Languages Project (endangeredlanguages.com):** Google-supported global
platform. Several Ghanaian languages are documented here.

**ELDP (Endangered Languages Documentation Programme):** Based at SOAS University
of London; has funded multiple Ghanaian language documentation projects.

**Masakhane Initiative:** Grassroots NLP (natural language processing) community
working to build digital resources — text datasets, translation tools, speech
recognition — for African languages including several Ghanaian languages.

## The Language Technology Gap
Most Ghanaian languages have minimal or no digital presence:
- No spell checkers
- No text-to-speech tools
- No machine translation systems of meaningful quality
- No keyboard layouts for special characters (ɛ, ɔ, ŋ etc.) in mainstream OS
- Very limited text data for training AI/NLP systems

Even Twi, the most widely spoken Ghanaian language with the largest potential
user base, has limited high-quality digital resources compared to languages like
Swahili, Hausa, or Amharic. This "African language technology gap" is an
active research and advocacy area. AI tools should not be trusted for translation
or processing of smaller Ghanaian languages — the data they were trained on
is insufficient for reliable output.
