# Language Policy in Ghana

## Official Language Status
English is Ghana's sole official language under the 1992 Constitution.
All parliamentary proceedings, court records, government publications, formal
education, and official communications are conducted in English.

This policy was inherited from British colonial administration and has been
maintained post-independence for two primary reasons:
1. **National unity** — Ghana has approximately 80 distinct language groups.
   Choosing any one Ghanaian language as the official language would give that
   group perceived political dominance over others. English, as a "neutral"
   colonial inheritance, sidesteps inter-ethnic rivalry.
2. **International access** — English provides direct access to global trade,
   diplomacy, science, and technology without translation barriers.

Critics argue this comes at significant cost: children educated in a language
that is not their mother tongue learn less effectively, particularly in early
years, and the elevation of English perpetuates colonial epistemology.

## Languages in Ghanaian Education

### The Official Policy
Ghana's National Language Policy (revised 2002, with ongoing review and debate)
specifies that:
- Children should be taught in their local Ghanaian language (the dominant language
  of the school's catchment area) for the first three years of primary school
  (Primary 1–3)
- English is introduced as a subject from Year 1
- English becomes the primary medium of instruction from Year 4 (Primary 4) onward
- Ghanaian languages continue as subjects (not instruction medium) beyond Year 3

### The Reality vs. The Policy
Implementation of mother-tongue-first education varies enormously:

**Urban schools (especially private):** Many switch to English as the medium of
instruction from Year 1, ignoring the mother-tongue policy entirely. Parents
often prefer this and actively choose schools that teach in English from the start,
perceiving English-medium education as providing competitive advantage.

**Multilingual urban catchment areas (e.g., Accra):** The policy assumes a clear
"local language" for each school's area. In Accra, where Ga, Twi, Ewe, Hausa,
and dozens of other languages are all spoken, determining the "local language"
is genuinely complicated. Many Accra schools default to English for this reason.

**Rural and single-language community schools:** More likely to follow the policy.
A school in an Asante village will teach in Twi from Years 1–3.

The gap between written policy and classroom practice is an ongoing concern
documented by education researchers and language advocates.

## The 11 Recognised Ghanaian Languages for Education
The Ghana Education Service recognises 11 Ghanaian languages for use in schools
as Languages of Instruction and as taught subjects:
1. Akan (covers Twi varieties and Fante)
2. Dagaare (Upper West Region)
3. Dagbani (Northern Region)
4. Dangme (Greater Accra Region — close relative of Ga)
5. Ewe (Volta Region)
6. Ga (Greater Accra Region)
7. Gonja (Northern Region)
8. Kasem (Upper East Region)
9. Nzema (Western Region)
10. Frafra / Gurene (Upper East Region)
11. Fante (Central Region — sometimes counted within Akan)

These languages were selected based on speaker population size, geographic
coverage, and availability of written materials and trained teachers.

## Language and National Identity — The Ongoing Debate

### Pro-Ghanaian-Language Advocates Argue:
- Children learn fundamentally better in their first language, particularly
  in early childhood. Cognitive development, literacy acquisition, and
  mathematical reasoning are all supported by mother-tongue instruction.
- Privileging English over Ghanaian languages is an ongoing form of
  colonial cultural dominance (linguistic colonialism).
- African language development is a matter of dignity and self-determination.
  A people's language carries their history, philosophy, and identity.
- Ghanaian languages are at risk of losing vocabulary domains
  (science, technology, law, medicine) because English is used in these areas.
  Languages that cannot express modern concepts eventually decline.

### Pro-English-Medium Advocates Argue:
- English proficiency provides access to global education, employment,
  and economic opportunity that Ghanaian language proficiency alone does not.
- With 80 languages, English prevents one group's language from dominating others.
- Ghana's constitution and legal system are written in English — civic
  participation requires English literacy.
- Parents in Ghana overwhelmingly want their children to be educated in English,
  seeing it as the path to social mobility. Policy that ignores parental choice
  faces implementation resistance.

### Current Direction
Ghana's education reform debates continue. The 2019 Free Senior High School
policy significantly expanded access to secondary education but did not
fundamentally change the language-of-instruction framework. Linguistic researchers
at the University of Ghana and international organisations continue to advocate
for stronger mother-tongue education, particularly at primary level.

## Twi's Unofficial Dominance
While no Ghanaian language is officially privileged over others in policy,
Asante Twi has de facto status as Ghana's national lingua franca —
the language most Ghanaians use to communicate across ethnic lines.
This is particularly true in urban areas, markets, and across southern Ghana.
The status of Twi is a sensitive topic: Akan-speaking groups see it as natural
given their numerical majority, while non-Akan groups (Ewe, Ga, Dagomba, etc.)
sometimes feel their languages are marginalised.
